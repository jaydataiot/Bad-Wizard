The Curious History of Malgrin the PuceThe Legend of the Bad WizardIf you ask Malgrin how old he is, he’ll narrow his eyes, tug thoughtfully on his beard, and reply,“Old enough to know that questions like that usually end badly.”No one has ever gotten a better answer.Most historians agree that Malgrin the Puce was born sometime during the late fifteenth century in a tiny stone cottage overlooking the Muttering Marsh, where the frogs complained louder than the villagers and the mosquitoes were rumored to have magical licenses.His father, Marvin the Beige, was a perfectly respectable wizard. Not a famous wizard. Not a legendary wizard. Simply dependable. If you needed a leaking roof enchanted against rain or a stubborn goat persuaded to stop eating your laundry, Marvin was your wizard.His mother, Loretta, was a brilliant hedge witch who brewed potions so effective that neighboring kingdoms traveled for weeks just to buy a single bottle. She believed every problem had a solution.She never anticipated that one of those problems would be her own son.The Boy with the BeardMost children eventually grow beards.Malgrin skipped the waiting.By the age of six he had already sprouted a magnificent, unruly beard that reached halfway down his chest.The village barber resigned.His classmates laughed endlessly.Not only was Malgrin the shortest child anyone had ever seen……he was also the only first-grader who needed beard oil.The insults came daily.“Pocket Wizard!”“Stump Beard!”“Garden Gnome!”“Grandpa Baby!”Malgrin pretended not to care.He cared tremendously.Not a Very Good WizardThe other children assumed Malgrin would become a great wizard because both of his parents were exceptionally talented.Unfortunately…Magic and Malgrin had a difficult relationship.His spells almost never did what they were supposed to.If he attempted to levitate a chair……the table floated instead.If he tried to turn a frog purple……the mayor’s horse suddenly knew opera.When instructed to summon rain……he once accidentally summoned seventeen extremely confused ducks.No one ever figured out where the ducks came from.He accidentally set the family barn on fire.Twice.Then accidentally extinguished it with strawberry pudding.He once enchanted his own trousers instead of his boots.They ran away.Life with Malgrin was never dull.It was simply exhausting.Escaping into BooksBecause he was constantly teased for both his height and his spectacular magical failures, Malgrin stopped spending time with other children.Instead, he escaped into the only place that never laughed at him.The library.He read constantly.History.Magic.Engineering.Poetry.Cookbooks.Maps.Instruction manuals.Anything.One rainy afternoon, while searching for somewhere quiet enough to avoid another humiliating broomstick lesson, Malgrin wandered behind a shelf that hadn’t been moved in centuries.There he discovered a hidden room.Inside were thousands of ancient wizard scrolls.Dust covered everything.Some scrolls were so old that even the spiders seemed afraid to crawl across them.For years, Malgrin secretly studied the forgotten writings hidden inside that room.Most described magical disciplines long abandoned.One collection described something almost no one had ever heard of.Pictomancy.The forgotten art of traveling through paintings.Unlike ordinary teleportation, pictomancy connected every painted image into a hidden magical world existing just beneath reality itself.Most scholars believed it impossible.Malgrin wasn’t convinced that it would work.Balthazar’s School of Wizardry and ShenanigansDespite his unimpressive spellcasting, Malgrin somehow earned admission to the legendary Balthazar’s School of Wizardry and Shenanigans.Its famous motto read:“Question Everything. Especially Authority.”The school quickly became both the happiest and most miserable years of Malgrin’s life.Academically…he was brilliant.He consistently finished at the top of every written examination.Spell Theory.Runic Mathematics.Potion Chemistry.Arcane Engineering.Magical History.Advanced Shenanigans.Theoretical Shenanigans.He understood magic better than almost anyone.Actually performing it…was another matter entirely.His practical examinations became legendary.Asked to levitate a candle…he accidentally levitated the examiner.Asked to summon a familiar…he somehow produced an irritated goose wearing spectacles.Asked to create a small magical light…he accidentally exploded three classroom windows.Again.The other students laughed constantly.They mocked his beard.They mocked his height.They mocked his spectacular inability to cast even simple spells correctly.Yet every night…those same students quietly knocked on his dormitory door.“Malgrin…can I borrow your notes?”“Malgrin…could you explain Chapter Twelve?”“Malgrin…what’s the answer to Question Four?”“Malgrin…mind if I sit beside you during tomorrow’s exam?”Malgrin knew exactly what they were doing.He let them anyway.Not because he liked them.Because correcting their mistakes was somehow easier than arguing with them.After years of persistence—and an astonishing number of accidental explosions—Malgrin finally graduated.Not because he had become a powerful wizard.Because he refused to quit.The Real WorldGraduation was supposed to solve everything.It didn’t.Every prestigious magical guild rejected him.Every royal court wanted experienced wizards.Every wealthy patron hired only famous names.Malgrin quickly discovered what every struggling young wizard already knew.The magical economy was rigged.Nearly all the wealth, influence, enchanted towers, dragon contracts, and prestigious commissions belonged to the top one percent of wizards.Everyone else competed for scraps.Eventually…Malgrin accepted the only job anyone would offer him.He became…a Fanny Puffer.The Lowest Job in Wizarding SocietyHis responsibilities were surprisingly demanding.Every morning he reported to royal banquets, festivals, and noble celebrations carrying:* one brass bellows,* one patch kit,* three rolls of enchanted tape,* an oversized belt,* an embarrassing hat,* and several emergency air valves.His duties included:Watching noble trousers for soft spots.Running after aristocrats whose fashionable puffed breeches began to deflate.Inflating ceremonial pants during royal dances.Making funny noises while pumping air to entertain children.Pretending to enjoy every minute of it.He had spent decades studying the deepest mysteries of magic……only to become the kingdom’s foremost expert in inflatable pants.The PaintingEventually the ridicule became too much.Malgrin withdrew from everyone.He stopped reading.Stopped inventing.Stopped caring.One evening, convinced that his life had amounted to little more than one magical disaster after another, he wandered aimlessly until he entered a forgotten gallery.There…hanging on a dusty wall…was the most extraordinary painting he had ever seen.It depicted an enormous woman seated upon a moss-covered throne deep within an enchanted forest.She happily devoured an enormous roasted turkey leg while tiny people danced joyfully around her feet.One tiny fellow carefully painted her toenails.Fairies drifted lazily through golden shafts of sunlight.Nearby, a curious half-man, half-sheep creature grazed peacefully beneath ancient trees.Everything about the painting radiated impossible warmth.Impossible joy.Impossible peace.Malgrin stared for hours.Then, without thinking…he whispered the forbidden pictomancy spell he had discovered years earlier among the forgotten scrolls beneath Balthazar’s library.He leaped.The painting rippled like water.Malgrin vanished.He was never seen again.The Garage SaleCenturies passed.Kingdoms rose and fell.Magic slowly faded from public memory.The painting changed hands countless times before eventually ending up at an ordinary garage sale.Most people walked past it without a second glance.Fortunately…the new founders of small creative studio did not.They bought the strange old painting simply because they thought it looked interesting.They hung it on the workshop wall.Late that evening, the painting began to emit a strange odor. One of the game designers swore she saw a puff of purple smoke burst from the canvas and drift lazily across the room. At first, no one believed her.Around midnight, with only a handful of people still working in the studio, an unsettling sound echoed through the building. It was unmistakable: the loud, irritable grumbling of someone’s bowels… coming from inside the painting.Everyone within earshot slowly gathered around the old canvas, exchanging confused and bewildered glances.“What in the world is that?” someone whispered.One member of the design team cautiously stepped forward. She raised a trembling hand and pressed it gently against the surface of the painting, hoping to sense whatever was causing the commotion.The instant her fingers touched the canvas, a tremendous blast of rancid air erupted from the painting.The room was immediately overwhelmed by a smell so indescribably foul that one witness later described it as “a spicy yak rolled in burnt cabbage and left to ferment in the summer sun.” Another simply fainted.Everyone stumbled backward, coughing and shielding their noses.When the air finally began to clear, they noticed something impossible.The enormous woman, the dancing little people, the fairies, and the half-man, half-sheep creature had all vanished.The painting no longer depicted a scene at all.Instead, the surface rippled like the waters of a moonlit lake.The glowing canvas shimmered and swirled, as though it had become a doorway rather than a painting.The designers locked eyes with one another but said nothing.Whatever was about to emerge had to be a monster.Or a demon.Perhaps some ancient evil imprisoned for centuries.Maybe an unspeakable horror from another realm.The anticipation became unbearable.Then…A ladder slowly emerged from the glowing canvas.Just… a ladder.It unfolded one rung at a time until it rested gently on the studio floor.A moment later, a tiny leather boot appeared on the top rung.Then another.The room fell completely silent.Slowly, a short, bearded wizard climbed down the ladder, grumbling under his breath the entire way.When he finally reached the floor, he brushed a bit of dust from his robe, looked around the workshop with obvious disappointment, and sighed.Then he glanced up at the painting and frowned.“Honestly,” he muttered, shaking his head. “Whoever hung this thing couldn’t use a level if their life depended on it.”It was the first thing anyone heard Malgrin the Puce say in hundreds of years.After his critique of the painting, he criticized the lighting.Declared the workshop hopelessly disorganized.Rearranged half the furniture.Corrected the rules of a prototype board game.Edited dialogue in a comic script.Sketched a better cover illustration.Then disappeared back into the painting before anyone had a chance to ask questions.The next morning he returned.Then the next.Eventually everyone simply accepted that the grumpy little wizard was part of the studio.No one hired him.No one could fire him.And, to this day, no one knows whether he actually works there or merely refuses to leave but the studio, now named after him, would not be the same without him.The Bad Wizard of Bad Wizard StudioThese days Malgrin serves as the studio’s unofficial mascot, resident curmudgeon, and accidental creative director.He curates board game ideas, playtests prototypes with merciless honesty, designs game-night experiences through Set the Scene, illustrates comic covers, restores ancient artwork, produces podcasts while complaining about microphones, appears in promotional videos despite insisting he hates cameras, and constantly wanders between paintings searching for new ideas.Ironically, he still isn’t a particularly good wizard.He occasionally teleports into the wrong painting.Sometimes he accidentally animates furniture.Every now and then he sets something on fire.The studio keeps three enchanted fire extinguishers nearby at all times.Just in case.Yet everyone at Bad Wizard Studio agrees on one thing.Malgrin may be a bad wizard……but he’s an exceptionally good creator and an essential part of the team.And perhaps that’s exactly the kind of magic the world needed all along.
